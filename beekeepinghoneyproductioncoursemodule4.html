📊 Common Bee Diseases & Pests in Africa

Disease/Pest Symptoms Prevention Treatment Impact Level
Varroa Mites Small mites on bees, deformed wings, shortened lifespan, viral diseases Regular monitoring, healthy diet, clean equipment Oxalic acid, formic acid, essential oils, drone removal Very High
American Foulbrood (AFB) Dead larvae in comb, foul smell, scale formation, rope-like consistency Good hive hygiene, avoid mixing colonies, sterilize tools No cure - cull colony, burn comb or use fumigants Critical
European Foulbrood (EFB) Dead larvae, sour smell, discolored comb, scattered brood pattern Remove diseased brood, good nutrition, strong colonies Remove frames with diseased larvae, requeening High
African Savanah Fly Weak colonies robbed of resources, larvae parasitized Maintain strong colonies, reduce entrance, remove dead bees Eliminate weak colonies, screen entrances, traps High
Nosema Disease Dysentery-like symptoms, sick bees unable to fly, colony decline Good nutrition, clean hives, adequate ventilation Antibiotic (fumagillin), requeening, comb replacement Moderate
Chalk Brood Mummified larvae turning white/chalk-like in comb Good ventilation, reduce humidity, strong colonies Remove infected comb, improve hive conditions Low-Moderate

📋 6 Implementation Steps

Step 1: Establish Health Monitoring Protocol
Create a regular inspection schedule
During active season (spring/summer), inspect each colony every 7-10 days. During dormant season, inspect every 2-3 weeks if needed. Create a checklist to assess: brood pattern (solid, scattered, spotty), adult bee population, food stores, and visible pests. Record observations in a log for each colony. Note colony strength (1-5 scale) and overall health status.
Step 2: Learn Disease Recognition
Study symptoms of common diseases in your region
Use the disease chart above to familiarize yourself with symptoms of the most common problems in your area. Consult with your local beekeeper association or agricultural extension office about which diseases are prevalent in your region. Collect photos/samples of diseased comb from experienced beekeepers if available. Practice identifying signs of varroa mites (reddish spots on bees), foulbrood (distinctive smell), and other regional diseases.
Step 3: Implement Biosecurity Measures
Prevent disease spread between colonies
Establish these practices: use separate equipment for each colony or sterilize between use, don't move equipment or bees between colonies without good reason, prevent robbing by proper hive spacing and entrance management, wear clean clothing when visiting hives, wash hands between colony inspections, disinfect tools (smoker, hive tool, gloves) regularly. If you find disease in one colony, work with it last and clean equipment afterward.
Step 4: Stock Emergency Treatment Supplies
Prepare for disease response
Contact suppliers (agricultural extension, beekeeping associations, online retailers) to identify available treatments for common diseases in your region. Stock supplies for varroa control (oxalic acid, formic acid, or approved alternatives), disinfectants for equipment (bleach solution, fire), replacement brood comb in case of AFB, and bee medications (if available/legal in your country). Ensure you understand proper application rates, timing, and safety protocols before needing to use treatments.
Step 5: Establish Disease Response Plan
Know what to do if you find disease
Create written procedures for different scenarios: if you find varroa mites, if you suspect foulbrood, if you notice weak colony symptoms, if you see African Savanah fly damage. Include: steps to confirm disease, notification to local authorities (if required), treatment plan or culling decision, isolation of affected colony, and cleanup procedures. Know which diseases require reporting to government agricultural authorities in your country.
Step 6: Join Local Beekeeper Network
Access expertise and stay informed
Connect with experienced beekeepers in your area through local associations, extension services, or informal networks. Share disease observations and treatment results. Attend workshops or training on disease management. Participate in local initiatives to monitor and control regional pest problems (e.g., coordinated varroa monitoring). Build relationships with veterinary services or extension specialists who can help diagnose unusual problems.

🚀 Real-World Project: Disease Management & Biosecurity Plan

Develop a comprehensive health and disease management program for your apiary.

Part 1: Regional Disease Risk Assessment

Research the diseases and pests most common in your specific region (consult local extension office, experienced beekeepers, agricultural ministry). Create a table listing: disease name, severity (low/moderate/high/critical), prevalence in your area (% of beekeepers affected), and season when most common. Prioritize the 3-4 most significant regional threats.

Part 2: Health Monitoring Schedule

Design a specific hive inspection schedule for your operation based on your climate and colony count. Create a calendar showing: inspection frequency for active season vs. dormant season, inspection duration, what you'll check for, and timeline for each colony. Include backup dates for emergencies. Create a simple inspection checklist form you'll use for each hive visit.

Part 3: Biosecurity Protocol

Document all biosecurity measures you'll implement to prevent disease spread: equipment management (separate vs. shared), cleaning/disinfection procedures, hive spacing, entrance management to prevent robbing, protective equipment (what you'll wear), hand washing protocol, and rules for moving bees/equipment between colonies. Create a step-by-step guide for cleaning and disinfecting equipment.

Part 4: Treatment & Supply Inventory

List all disease treatments and preventive supplies available in your region for your top 3 disease threats. For each treatment, research and document: product name, active ingredient, dosage/application rate, cost per unit, how to order, required safety precautions, and effectiveness. Create a supplies checklist and reordering plan.

Part 5: Disease Recognition Guide

Create a visual guide for identifying signs of disease in your colonies. For each major disease: describe symptoms in detail, note at what stage of colony development it appears, sketch or describe what affected comb looks like, list any distinctive smells, and describe affected bee appearance. Use photos if available. Include notes on how to confirm diagnosis (e.g., which symptoms are definitive vs. just suggestive).

Part 6: Emergency Response Procedures

Document step-by-step procedures for four scenarios: (1) Finding varroa mites, (2) Suspecting foulbrood, (3) Encountering weak colony with multiple symptoms, (4) Finding African Savanah fly damage. For each, include: confirmation steps, isolation measures, treatment decisions, timeline, who to notify, and cleanup procedures. Include decision trees for whether to treat or cull.

Part 7: Record Keeping System

Design your health record-keeping system (paper or digital). Include: hive inspection form to record observations, disease log to track any illness detected, treatment log showing what was applied and results, medication inventory tracking, and disposal records for culled colonies. Create templates for each form and explain how you'll organize and review the data.

Part 8: Local Network & Knowledge Plan

Identify local beekeeping resources you'll connect with: beekeeper associations, agricultural extension office contacts, experienced mentor beekeepers, veterinary services, and online forums/communities. For each, document: contact information, what expertise they offer, how often you'll communicate, and how they'll help your disease management efforts. Schedule your first meeting/communication date.
